# Beyond Explainable AI (XAI): An Overdue Paradigm Shift and Post-XAI Research Directions

**ArXiv ID:** [2602.24176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24176)  
**Authors:** Saleh Afroogh, Ali Akbari, Emmie Malone, Mostafa Rezaei, Emad Doroozchi, and 44 additional authors (cross-disciplinary team)  
**Submitted:** February 27, 2026 (revised April 10, 2026)  
**Subfield:** Human-Centered Explainability  

---

## Executive Summary

This ambitious cross-disciplinary paper argues that the entire Explainable AI (XAI) research program is fundamentally broken — not merely in need of incremental improvement, but suffering from deep paradoxes, conceptual confusions, and false assumptions that make the current paradigm incapable of achieving its stated goals. Drawing on philosophy of science, cognitive psychology, human-computer interaction, and machine learning, the authors identify two core paradoxes and five false assumptions that are embedded in the XAI enterprise, then propose a four-pronged post-XAI paradigm shift as a constructive alternative. The paper is notable both as a rigorous intellectual critique of the field and as a forward-looking agenda for what trustworthy AI transparency should actually look like.

---

## Problem Statement

### The XAI Crisis

Explainable AI has been one of the most active and well-funded research areas in ML for the past decade. Tools like LIME, SHAP, GradCAM, and concept-based explanations have been deployed in production systems, used in regulatory compliance, and cited in thousands of papers. Yet a growing body of evidence suggests these methods may be fundamentally inadequate:

- **Faithfulness problems:** Explanations generated by post-hoc methods (SHAP, LIME) often do not faithfully reflect the actual decision process of the model. Studies show that SHAP explanations can be manipulated without changing model behavior, and that different explanation methods give contradictory attributions for the same prediction.

- **Human understanding gap:** Empirical studies consistently find that users who receive XAI explanations do not understand model behavior significantly better than those who don't. The explanations are cognitively plausible but informationally vacuous.

- **Evaluation circularity:** XAI methods are typically evaluated by metrics that measure agreement with other XAI methods (faithfulness to a proxy, agreement with human intuitions about which features "should" matter), creating circular validation.

- **Deployment paradox:** Methods developed for interpretability (decision trees, linear models) are less accurate, but accurate black-box models are only explainable post-hoc. The post-hoc explanations of accurate models are unfaithful; the faithful models are inaccurate.

### What Prior Approaches Miss

The paper argues that the XAI community has been treating symptoms rather than causes:

1. **Better approximations of bad explanations:** Improving SHAP's approximation quality doesn't address the fundamental question of whether SHAP value attribution is the right notion of explanation.

2. **User studies with wrong questions:** HCI research on XAI typically asks "which explanation do users prefer?" rather than "does this explanation help users make better decisions?"

3. **Regulatory compliance theater:** GDPR's "right to explanation" has led to the production of explanations that satisfy legal requirements without providing genuine understanding — a form of explainability washing.

## Core Concepts & Theory

### The Two Paradoxes of XAI

**Paradox 1: The Comprehensibility-Faithfulness Paradox**

Explanations face an irreducible tension:
- To be **faithful** to the model, an explanation must capture the actual computation — which for deep neural networks involves billions of floating-point operations, attention patterns across thousands of tokens, and nonlinear feature interactions.
- To be **comprehensible** to humans, an explanation must be simplified to a small number of features or a few sentences — a radical compression that inevitably loses information.

A faithful explanation of a neural network is incomprehensible; a comprehensible explanation is unfaithful. The paper argues this is not a technical limitation to be solved by better methods — it is a **logical paradox** inherent to the goals of XAI.

Formally, let $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ be a complex model and $e: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{E}$ an explanation function mapping inputs to human-comprehensible explanation space $\mathcal{E}$. For comprehensibility, $|\mathcal{E}|$ must be small (bounded by working memory limitations, typically 7 ± 2 chunks). For faithfulness, $e$ must preserve the information content of $f$, i.e., $H(f(X) | e(X)) \approx 0$. But $f$ has vast information content while $\mathcal{E}$ has bounded capacity — hence both conditions cannot hold simultaneously.

**Paradox 2: The Oversight-Autonomy Paradox**

XAI is justified on the grounds that humans should be able to oversee AI decisions. But:
- If the AI is **correct** most of the time (why deploy it otherwise?), human oversight based on explanations will mostly rubberstamp AI decisions, providing false assurance of genuine oversight.
- If the AI is **wrong** in subtle ways (the cases where oversight matters most), the explanation of a flawed decision will appear locally coherent — the model will generate a plausible-sounding explanation for an incorrect conclusion.

Explanations are most useful when we already know when to distrust the model; when we don't know this, explanations cannot reliably flag problematic cases.

### The Five False Assumptions

The paper catalogs five assumptions embedded in the XAI research program that are empirically false or theoretically unjustified:

1. **The Transparency Assumption:** That making model internals visible to users leads to understanding. *Falsehood:* Cognitive science shows humans cannot process the raw information in model weights or even simplified feature attributions without domain expertise.

2. **The Proxy Fidelity Assumption:** That post-hoc explanations are faithful proxies for model behavior. *Falsehood:* Multiple studies show explanations can be adversarially manipulated without changing predictions; explanation agreement across methods is low.

3. **The User-Agnostic Assumption:** That there exists a single "good" explanation applicable to all users (domain experts, regulators, affected individuals). *Falsehood:* What constitutes a useful explanation is radically context- and user-dependent.

4. **The Sufficient Statistics Assumption:** That feature importance scores capture the causally relevant information in model decisions. *Falsehood:* Feature importance is not causal; a high SHAP value for a feature does not mean the feature caused the prediction (correlation ≠ causation).

5. **The Completability Assumption:** That XAI can, in principle, be completed — that there is a solution to the comprehensibility-faithfulness paradox waiting to be found. *Falsehood:* The paradox is logical, not technical.

## Main Ideas & Key Contributions

### The Four-Pronged Post-XAI Paradigm

The paper's constructive contribution is a four-part agenda for what should replace the XAI program:

**1. Verification-Focused Interactive AI (IAI)**

Rather than explaining model decisions post-hoc, IAI establishes **scientific community protocols for certifying AI system performance** in specific deployment contexts. This draws on the analogy of how the scientific community certifies drugs (RCTs, pre-specified outcome measures, regulatory review) rather than explaining the mechanism of drug action to patients.

Key elements:
- Pre-deployment behavioral specifications defining acceptable performance bounds
- Red-teaming and adversarial testing as rigorous performance certification
- Ongoing monitoring with pre-specified drift thresholds
- *Explanation* of AI decisions is replaced by *certification* of AI behavior

**2. AI Epistemology**

This pillar calls for rigorous philosophical foundations for questions like: What would it mean for an AI system to "know" something? What are the appropriate epistemic standards for AI-generated knowledge claims? How should AI uncertainty be communicated and calibrated?

Specific proposals:
- Mandatory uncertainty quantification alongside all AI outputs
- Calibration audits as a standard deployment requirement
- Formalization of knowledge representation in AI systems beyond statistical correlation

**3. User-Sensible AI (USAI)**

USAI replaces the user-agnostic explanation with context-sensitive communication:
- **Expert users** (radiologists, lawyers): Receive technical model uncertainty and performance bounds
- **Affected individuals** (loan applicants, patients): Receive actionable recourse information ("Here's what you can do to get a different decision") rather than feature attribution
- **Regulators:** Receive auditable performance certifications rather than example explanations

The key insight is that different stakeholders need different *kinds* of information about AI, not different presentations of the same feature attribution.

**4. Model-Centered Interpretability (MCI)**

This is the only pillar that resembles traditional XAI. MCI focuses on **mechanistic, faithful analysis of model internals** — specifically mechanistic interpretability research (circuit discovery, SAE analysis, probing) rather than post-hoc approximations.

MCI explicitly excludes:
- LIME, SHAP, and gradient-based saliency (too unfaithful)
- Human-comprehensibility as a primary constraint (faithfulness trumps comprehensibility)
- Application to production decisions without separate certification (MCI results are research tools, not user-facing explanations)

### The Critique of Regulatory XAI

A significant portion of the paper analyzes how GDPR Article 22, the EU AI Act, and FDA guidance on AI/ML-based SaMD have operationalized XAI requirements in ways that enable compliance theater:

- **GDPR's "meaningful information"** requirement has been interpreted to allow feature attribution summaries, which the paper argues provide no meaningful understanding.
- **EU AI Act's transparency requirements** focus on documentation and logging rather than genuine user comprehension.
- The paper recommends replacing explanation requirements with **behavioral certification requirements** — performance bounds, stress test results, and monitoring protocols.

## Methodology & Implementation

### Analytical Approach

Unlike empirical ML papers, this work uses:
- **Systematic literature review:** Analysis of 400+ XAI papers to identify common assumptions
- **Philosophical analysis:** Application of philosophy of science (Popper's falsifiability, Kuhn's paradigm shifts) to XAI claims
- **Cognitive science synthesis:** Integration of working memory limits, mental model formation, and decision-making literature
- **Legal analysis:** Review of GDPR, EU AI Act, and FDA guidance interpretations

### Evidence for the Paradoxes

**Faithfulness evidence:**
- Heo et al. (2019): SHAP explanations can be manipulated to hide bias while preserving predictions
- Adebayo et al. (2018): Gradient saliency maps are often insensitive to model parameters (identical maps for random and trained models)
- Krishna et al. (2022): 10+ different XAI methods give low inter-method agreement ($r < 0.4$ pairwise correlations for feature rankings)

**Comprehension evidence:**
- Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. (2021): Users who received linear model explanations did not make better predictions about model behavior than those without explanations
- Bansal et al. (2021): Explanations sometimes *decrease* appropriate reliance (users override correct AI decisions more when given explanations)
- Wang & Yin (2021): Mental models of AI formed from XAI explanations are systematically biased

### Limitations

- The paper is primarily theoretical and rhetorical; it does not present experimental evidence for the proposed post-XAI alternatives.
- The critique focuses on post-hoc explanation methods (LIME, SHAP) and is less directly applicable to inherently interpretable models.
- The proposed alternatives (IAI, AI Epistemology) are high-level research agendas, not ready-to-deploy solutions.
- Some practitioners may find the critique overstated for narrowly scoped, well-tested domains.

## Practical Applications & Real-World Use Cases

### Healthcare

The paper's User-Sensible AI framework is directly applicable:
- **For radiologists:** Provide calibrated uncertainty intervals ("85% confidence, 12% false negative rate on this scan type") rather than saliency maps
- **For patients:** Provide actionable recourse ("A follow-up CT scan in 6 months would update this assessment") rather than feature attributions
- **For regulators:** Provide prospective validation results on diverse populations rather than example explanations

### Financial Services

Under the post-XAI framework:
- Credit decisions are certified via pre-deployment fairness testing and ongoing monitoring, not justified via SHAP values
- Adverse action notices provide actionable steps to improve creditworthiness rather than "your income was the most important factor"
- Regulators receive performance audits across demographic segments rather than explanation samples

### Legal and Criminal Justice

The Oversight-Autonomy Paradox is most acute in high-stakes legal contexts (recidivism risk scoring, facial recognition in law enforcement):
- Judges or lawyers given SHAP explanations of a risk score tend to defer to it *more*, not less — increasing algorithmic authority while providing an illusion of human oversight
- The behavioral certification alternative would require pre-specified performance bounds by demographic group before deployment, with mandatory human review of edge cases outside the model's validated operating range

### Compliance and Regulatory Implications

The paper's regulatory analysis has immediate implications for organizations deploying AI under the EU AI Act:
- **High-risk AI providers** should consult this paper to understand the limits of current XAI methods as compliance evidence
- **Regulators** should consider whether current AI Act technical documentation requirements are sufficient or whether behavioral certification standards are needed
- **GDPR controllers** deploying automated decision-making should review whether their current "meaningful information" disclosures satisfy the spirit vs. merely the letter of Article 22

## Insights & Implications

### Broader Implications for Trustworthy AI

This paper raises fundamental questions about whether the AI research community has been pursuing the right goals in the explainability space. Its central claim — that XAI as currently conceived cannot achieve its stated objectives — is both uncomfortable and generative. If correct, it implies that:

1. Billions of dollars invested in XAI tooling and research are producing tools that cannot do what they promise
2. Regulatory frameworks built on XAI assumptions need fundamental revision
3. The xAI research agenda needs to split into (a) mechanistic understanding for researchers and (b) behavioral certification for practitioners

### State-of-the-Art Impact

This is one of the most cited paradigm-challenge papers in the XAI field since Lipton (2018) "The Mythos of Model Interpretability." Its cross-disciplinary authorship (including philosophers, cognitive scientists, legal scholars, and ML researchers) gives it unusual breadth and credibility.

### Open Questions and Debates

1. **Is the paradox really paradoxical?** Some researchers argue that partial faithfulness with bounded approximation error is sufficient for practical purposes — the paradox is one of degree rather than kind.
2. **Can mechanistic interpretability escape the paradox?** The paper's MCI pillar is the most technically promising, but even circuit-level explanations may be incomprehensible to non-expert stakeholders.
3. **Is behavioral certification sufficient for trust?** A fully certified but opaque system still provides no recourse or understanding to affected individuals — arguably a different kind of democratic deficit.
4. **Can the four pillars coexist?** Organizing the post-XAI agenda into four separate research communities risks fragmentation.

### Influence on xAI Research Directions

The paper is accelerating a shift toward:
- **Auditing-first approaches** that prioritize behavioral guarantees over explanations
- **Recourse-based methods** that give users actionable alternatives rather than passive attributions
- **Mechanistic safety research** as the legitimate heir to XAI's scientific goals
- **Epistemic humility** in AI communication — communicating uncertainty alongside predictions

## Related Work & Context

### Foundational Critiques This Builds On

- **Lipton (2018) "The Mythos of Model Interpretability":** Early critique of the conflation of different notions of interpretability; this paper extends and radicalizes that critique
- **Rudin (2019) "Stop Explaining Black Box Machine Learning Models for High Stakes Decisions":** Argues for inherently interpretable models; this paper goes further in critiquing even inherently interpretable models' explanations
- **Adebayo et al. (2018) "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps":** Empirical evidence for saliency map unfaithfulness cited extensively in this paper

### Relation to Papers in This Repository

- **Explaining AI Without Code: User Study (2026-02-01):** Empirically studies user interaction with XAI tools; this paper provides theoretical framing for why such studies often show limited benefit from explanations
- **XAI Methods and EU AI Act Requirements (2026-04-18):** Catalogues XAI methods that comply with the EU AI Act; this paper challenges whether compliance via explanations is meaningful, suggesting regulatory reform is needed

### Future Directions

- **Post-XAI empirical work:** Testing whether IAI (behavioral certification) approaches outperform XAI (explanation-based) approaches on user decision quality
- **Legal reform proposals:** Specific legislative language for certification-based AI oversight
- **Recourse as a right:** Formalizing actionable recourse as a legal entitlement distinct from explanation
- **Unified AI epistemology:** Developing formal frameworks for AI knowledge claims and uncertainty communication

### Connection to xAI Communities

This paper challenges the foundations of the LIME/SHAP community, while the authors align themselves with:
- The **mechanistic interpretability** community (their MCI pillar)
- The **fairness/auditing** community (behavioral certification)
- The **human factors** community (User-Sensible AI)
- The **formal verification** community (IAI's certification approach)
