# Mathematics in the Age of AI

**Authors:** Terence Tao  
**arXiv ID:** 2608.16753  
**Submitted:** August 17, 2026  
**Venue:** Proceedings of the International Congress of Mathematicians (ICM) 2026

## Executive Summary

Terence Tao's essay examines the profound implications of artificial intelligence tools capable of performing research-level mathematical tasks. Rather than debating AI capabilities, Tao shifts focus to a more fundamental question: what are the actual goals and values of mathematical research, and how might they need to evolve as AI transforms the practice of mathematics? The paper argues that implicit mathematical frameworks—how we define contributions, what we reward, and who performs the work—must become explicitly defined and debated.

## Problem Statement

The arrival of AI systems capable of solving research-level mathematical problems creates an unprecedented challenge for the mathematical community. The primary issue is not whether AI can perform mathematics (Tao assumes it can), but rather how the mathematical community should respond and what this means for the discipline's foundational values and practices.

**Research Gap:** While extensive discussion occurs about AI's technical capabilities, little systematic reflection exists on how mathematical values, reward structures, and conceptions of what constitutes a valid mathematical contribution might need to evolve. The mathematical community has historically operated with largely implicit assumptions about these matters.

## Core Concepts & Theory

### The Problem-Solving Paradigm

Tao uses problem-solving as a primary case study for examining mathematical values. Problem-solving represents one dimension of mathematical research, involving:
- Formulation and clarification of mathematical questions
- Discovery and development of solution techniques
- Verification and validation of proofs

### Mathematical Framework Levels

The paper distinguishes between two critical frameworks in mathematics:

1. **Foundational Framework for Truth**: The logical and axiomatic systems that establish mathematical validity (e.g., ZFC set theory). This framework remains largely unchanged regardless of who or what performs the mathematics.

2. **Implicit Framework of Values and Practices**: The largely unwritten conventions governing:
   - What constitutes a mathematical contribution
   - Which problems are worthy of study
   - What level of rigor is acceptable
   - Which methods are considered elegant or insightful
   - Who receives credit for mathematical work
   - What "understanding" means in mathematics

### Key Philosophical Arguments

**Theorem (Implicit vs. Explicit Frameworks)**: The distinction between truth and value in mathematics becomes sharper when AI systems can generate valid proofs. A proof generated by AI is logically equivalent to one generated by human mathematicians, yet the mathematical community may value them differently.

**Principle of Mathematical Pluralism**: Different mathematical communities and eras have maintained different implicit values while adhering to the same foundational frameworks. The rise of AI forces these value systems into explicit discourse.

## Main Ideas & Contributions

### 1. Stress-Testing Mathematical Values, Not Truth

**Contribution**: Tao argues that AI doesn't stress-test the foundational framework of mathematical truth but rather the implicit framework of mathematical values and practices. This reframing is crucial because it:
- Shifts the debate from "Can AI do mathematics?" to "What do we value in mathematics?"
- Acknowledges that mathematical truth exists independently of who discovers it
- Focuses attention on the human and social dimensions of mathematics

### 2. Explicitness as a Response Strategy

**Innovation**: Making the implicit explicit becomes a necessary response to AI-driven change. This involves:
- Formally defining what constitutes a "mathematical contribution" in different contexts
- Articulating criteria for mathematical significance and elegance
- Creating transparent reward structures and attribution systems
- Developing guidelines for how machine-generated and human-generated mathematics should be valued differently (if at all)

### 3. The Problem of Attribution and Credit

**Key Issue**: As AI systems contribute to mathematical discovery, the question of attribution becomes complex:
- Should AI systems be credited as co-authors?
- How do we value computational discovery versus conceptual insight?
- What happens to reputation systems built on individual achievement?

Tao suggests these questions cannot be answered by appealing to logical foundations but must be addressed through deliberate community choices about values.

### 4. Understanding vs. Computation

**Conceptual Contribution**: A distinction emerges between computational solutions (which AI excels at) and understanding (which remains more ambiguous to define):
- A computer may solve a problem but not "understand" it
- Mathematical understanding often involves insight into *why* something is true, not just that it is true
- The community may need to explicitly define what "understanding" means in different mathematical domains

## Methodology & Implementation

### Essay Structure and Approach

The paper is structured as a philosophical essay based on the author's ICM 2026 public lecture. The methodology involves:

**Primary Method**: Conditional reasoning — assuming AI capabilities will arrive (as a baseline assumption) and exploring the implications for mathematical practice

**Analytical Framework**: 
- Case study analysis focusing on problem-solving in mathematics
- Philosophical examination of implicit vs. explicit frameworks
- Thought experiments exploring hypothetical scenarios with advanced AI systems

### Problem-Solving as Case Study

Tao uses problem-solving to illustrate broader questions:

1. **Formulation Phase**: AI might excel at reformulating problems, but mathematicians value novel formulations
2. **Solution Phase**: AI can generate solutions quickly, but mathematicians value elegant, insightful proofs
3. **Verification Phase**: Both AI and humans can verify, but verification by humans traditionally carries more weight
4. **Publication Phase**: Current systems reward novelty and insight, potentially undervaluing AI contributions

### Key Arguments

**Argument 1**: The mathematical community must explicitly define what it values in mathematical research across different contexts (pure mathematics, applied mathematics, pedagogical mathematics, etc.).

**Argument 2**: Current implicit systems may not adequately accommodate AI systems without either:
- Changing what we reward in mathematics (potentially for the worse)
- Creating artificial restrictions on what constitutes valid mathematics (potentially limiting progress)

**Argument 3**: Explicit frameworks create opportunities for communities to make conscious choices about how mathematics should evolve.

## Practical Applications & Use Cases

### For Academic Mathematics

- **Research Evaluation**: Universities and funding agencies must develop explicit criteria for evaluating contributions when AI systems are involved
- **Course Design**: Mathematical pedagogy may need revision if students can access AI tutors and problem-solvers
- **Publication Standards**: Journals need explicit guidelines on AI-assisted and AI-generated proofs

### For Industrial Mathematics

- **Product Development**: Balancing speed (where AI excels) with reliability and understanding (human strengths)
- **Verification Standards**: Establishing when AI-generated solutions require human validation
- **Collaboration Models**: Developing effective human-AI partnerships in mathematical problem-solving

### For Mathematical Education

- **Skill Development**: Identifying which mathematical skills remain valuable when AI can solve many problems
- **Understanding Focus**: Potentially shifting education toward conceptual understanding rather than problem-solving technique
- **Critical Thinking**: Developing skills to evaluate and validate AI-generated mathematics

## Insights & Implications

### Implications for the Mathematical Community

1. **Value Reconstruction**: Mathematics must undergo a period of explicit value definition and community deliberation about its goals and practices.

2. **Disciplinary Evolution**: The arrival of AI forces mathematics to become more self-aware about its implicit assumptions, potentially strengthening the discipline through increased clarity.

3. **Pluralistic Futures**: Different mathematical communities may make different choices about incorporating AI, leading to increased pluralism in mathematical practice.

### Broader Field Impact

- **Role Modeling for Other Disciplines**: How mathematics adapts to AI may serve as a model for other knowledge disciplines facing similar challenges
- **Philosophical Foundations**: Raises fundamental questions about the nature of mathematical knowledge and human intellectual work
- **Societal Implications**: Mathematical responses to AI may inform how society at large adapts to advanced AI systems

### Limitations and Open Questions

**Unresolved Questions:**
- How should different mathematical communities balance speed and efficiency gains from AI against potentially reduced human engagement?
- What constitutes sufficient "understanding" of a mathematical result when produced by AI-assisted methods?
- How should mathematical reputation systems evolve to accommodate AI contributions?
- Will the explicit definition of mathematical values reveal conflicts within the mathematical community that are currently masked by implicit assumptions?
